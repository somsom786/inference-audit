The 60-second executive takeaway

Why GPU budgets go wrong in both directions.

1. Hidden headroom

Defaults leave real performance unused

Generic auto-fit settings ignore model-specific structure. On Inkling-2-Mini, moving only the 3×-looped layers to VRAM beat default auto-fit by ~37% decode, ~55% 8K prefill, and ~68% faster startup.

2. Misleading metrics

Raw tok/s can rise while users wait longer

Adding serving slots on Qwen3-8B raised burst tok/s by 57.7% and halved first-token time—yet pushed completion tails past deadlines and reduced correct on-time answers.

3. The decision rule

Qualify on your workload before buying

An optimization only creates financial value when it increases qualified throughput—correct answers delivered inside your latency and context targets—on your real traffic mix.

Study 1 · Architecture-aware placement

Inkling-2-Mini: ~37% faster decode and ~55% faster prefill on the same GPU.

Modern Mixture-of-Experts models frequently exceed a single card's VRAM. Standard offloading spreads weights sequentially, spending scarce GPU memory on layers that run once per token.

Inkling-2-Mini (26B, 16.6 GB Q4) loops 12 middle blocks three times per token—so those 6.88 GB carry 72.3% of all active expert traffic. By pinning 11 of those looped blocks on CUDA0 and keeping boundary experts in pinned host RAM (--load-mode none, -ub 512), we outperformed llama.cpp's own auto-fit across two interleaved runs while passing 6 of 6 local acceptance checks (load, chat, reasoning, counterfactual tools, 85% context retrieval, and 30 s sustained speed).

Scope: Single stream, 16K f16 KV, text only, Windows 11. Not an officially accepted local-ai-registry recipe; vision, broader quality, production concurrency, and customer infrastructure remain unverified.

Study 2 · Speculative decoding & its concurrency boundary

Forge Qwen3.5-9B: when a real single-user speedup stops scaling.

Native multi-token prediction (MTP) drafts candidate tokens ahead of the main model. Testing draft lengths k=1..4 across single-stream and concurrent panels exposed exactly where speculation helps—and where it hurts.

Where k=1 won (single stream)

Up to +23.5% faster code, 19/19 exact matches

With one draft token (k=1), repeat-bracket code decode rose +7.0% (71.56–71.57 → 76.60 tok/s). In the extended sweep, structured Python rose +23.5% (89.9 vs 72.8 tok/s) and 4K-context code rose +16.5% (78.6 vs 67.5 tok/s), with 19/19 byte-for-byte identical greedy outputs.

Open Forge MTP experiment

Where it failed (k≥2 & concurrency 2)

No gain under concurrency, +522 MiB extra VRAM

Draft lengths k=2, 3, 4 monotonically regressed (k=4 lost 22% on code and 29% on prose), and novel prose stayed flat even at k=1. Most importantly, at concurrency=2, k=1 failed to beat interleaved controls (115.9 vs 111.1–121.9 pooled tok/s) while consuming +522 MiB extra VRAM.

Studies 3 & 4 · Throughput vs useful capacity & endurance

Why counting tokens alone misleads capacity planning.

Qwen3-8B · 2 vs 4 serving slots

+57.7% chat burst tok/s, fewer answers on time

Doubling serving slots lifted homogeneous chat-burst output from 143.77 to 226.70 tok/s (+57.7%) while halving per-slot context from 8,192 to 4,096. Yet on a fresh mixed trace (1.15 offers/s), two slots delivered 315–317 correct on-time answers out of 345 versus only 284–293 on four slots—because p95 completion time stretched from 3.7–3.8 s to 4.5–4.7 s even as p95 TTFT fell to 0.75 s.

Burst evidence · Mixed-workload evidence

Qwen3-8B · Six-hour endurance soak

Zero runtime crashes, failed agent correctness

Across six continuous hours, the server completed 13,926 transactions with zero transport errors and 13,155 correct on-time completions overall. Breaking results down by workload family exposed a hidden failure: only 776 of 1,392 older agent tasks passed strict correctness—proving uptime and throughput hide application-level failure modes.
