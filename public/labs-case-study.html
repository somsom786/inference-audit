Executive case study / RTX 4070

More inference, honestly measured.

One consumer GPU, four experiments. Two produced large verified gains, two showed exactly where a gain stops. Together they show why a GPU budget question needs a measurement before a purchase.

Measured by Inference Audit Labs

Win one: architecture-aware placement.

Inkling-2-Mini reuses 12 blocks three times per token. Placing 11 of those looped blocks on the GPU, rather than letting llama.cpp auto-fit decide, raised decode by +36.5% to +37.8% and 8K prefill by +54.8% to +55.2%. It was 2.1x faster at decode and 8.1–8.2x faster at prefill than offloading all experts with --cpu-moe, and passed 6 of 6 acceptance gates. The lesson: knowing the model architecture can be worth more than a hardware upgrade. Read the Inkling experiment

Win two, with a boundary: one draft token.

On Forge Qwen3.5-9B, native speculation with one draft token raised single-stream code decode from 71.56–71.57 to 76.60 tok/s (+7.0%). The extended sweep confirmed k=1 as the optimum, with structured Python up 23.5% and 4K-context code up 16.5%, and 19 of 19 outputs matching byte for byte. But at concurrency 2 the gain vanished and VRAM rose by 522 MiB. A single-stream win is not a multi-user win. See the Forge experiment

Measured by Inference Audit Labs

The negative result: counting tokens alone.

On a homogeneous chat burst, increasing Qwen3-8B from two slots to four raised valid output from 143.77 to 226.70 tok/s (+57.7%). The test used the same RTX 4070, Q4_K_M, llama.cpp CUDA b12427 and a 16,384-position f16 KV pool. Each launch offered 48 requests, with three controls and two candidates. Evidence and denominator

On a fresh mixed trace, two slots completed 315–317 of 345 offers correctly and on time, versus 284–293 on four slots. First-token p95 fell from 1.616–1.741 s to 0.753 s, while completion p95 rose from 3.714–3.820 s to 4.523–4.678 s. These are ranges across two fresh launches per profile at 1.15 offers/s. Both missed the 95% per-family contract somewhere on the tested grid. Matched-run evidence

A fixed cache budget gave each of the four slots half the context allowance. Workloads that need more than one four-slot window require a separate qualification.

Six hours exposed a different limit.

The completed Qwen3-8B endurance archive contained 13,926 completed transactions and 13,155 correct on time. Only 776 of 1,392 older agent tasks were correct. The runtime stayed up for the scheduled study, but that agent workload still failed its correctness threshold. Download the aggregate recount

Published external research (Sybil Solutions)

The wider ecosystem points the same way.

Independent open-source work by Sybil Solutions reports gains on very different hardware: Intel Arc B390 256K prefill from 297 to 628 tok/s, an Arc Pro B70 at 91.2 tok/s on vLLM against 25.0 tok/s on llama.cpp in a cross-stack comparison, an RTX 3090 rising from 45.5 to 65.6 tok/s with multi-token prediction, and a 4x H200 NVL deployment moving from crashing to 229.1 tok/s.

Hypothetical economic scenario

Where the bill can change

A lower cost per answer requires a gain in correct work finished on time for your own workload. Even then, the invoice changes only when GPU hours or provisioned capacity can actually fall. Illustratively, at $50,000/month, a 30% qualified-throughput gain means 23.1% fewer GPU-hours, up to about $138,462/year if 100% of the spend can scale down. None of the experiments above established a deployed capacity or a customer saving.

What we’d test next

The studies establish what is possible and where it stops. A staging pilot would repeat the tests with your representative code, prose and agent workloads, together with your concurrency and latency requirements. That would show whether a gain reaches customers or changes the capacity you need to provision.
